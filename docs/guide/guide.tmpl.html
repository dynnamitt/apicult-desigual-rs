0 · Terms

The words this page uses for the parts of the grid:

{{f0}}
cell
One hexagon of the grid: a HexCell { hex, height, radius }. Its hex is the hexx coordinate, axial (q, r). This page says cell for the grid element and Hex only for the coordinate type.
centre
The cell's world position, a Vec2(x, z). Centres sit on a fixed lattice, √3 · nominal_hex_radius apart. Height is y, so 3D points are Vec3(x, height, z).
nominal radius
nominal_hex_radius in HGridSettings (default 4): the corner radius at which neighbouring faces would just touch. It fixes the lattice. Each cell's own radius comes from noise, between min_radius_ratio and max_radius_ratio times it (0.05 and 0.9 by default).
corner
One of the six points at distance cell.radius from the centre, in the directions of a unit hexagon's corners, at the cell's height, numbered 0–5 (hexx VertexDirection).
edge
The side from corner i to corner i+1, numbered 0–5 (hexx EdgeDirection). Edge e faces the neighbour in direction e; the neighbour sees the same edge as (e+3) mod 6.
face
The flat top of a cell, drawn as a fan of six triangles from the centre.
gap
The space between faces, which opens wherever cell.radius < nominal_hex_radius. With the default ratios that is every cell; a max_radius_ratio above 1, or a radius pinned by an override, can make faces touch or overlap. The gap is filled by two kinds of piece:
gap quad
Across an edge two cells share: two corners from each, drawn as two triangles.
junction triangle
Where three cells meet: one corner from each.
owner
The one cell that emits a gap piece, by the rules below.
ring k
The cells at distance exactly k from a given cell (hexx Hex::ring); a radius-r grid is rings 0 to r.

Two ownership rules keep every gap piece from being drawn twice. 1.5, 1.6 and sections 3 to 5 all rest on them:

  • Gap quads. Every interior edge is shared by two cells. If one meets it as an even edge (0, 2, 4), the other meets it as odd (3, 5, 1), because e+3 flips parity. So "the cell that meets it as an even edge owns it" picks exactly one owner.
  • Junction triangles. Every interior corner is shared by three cells, and it is corner 0 or 1 of exactly one of them. That cell owns the triangle, so each cell owns the junctions at its corners 0 and 1.

A gap piece is only emitted when every cell it touches exists. Later sections add entangled cells (section 4) and chunks and seams (section 5).

1 · The math functions, simplest first

src/math.rs holds pure functions over glam and hexx types. None of them know about HGridLayout. They're ordered here from plain arithmetic to the grid combinatorics the rest of the crate is built on.

1.1 map_noise_to_rangelinear rescalenoise in [−1, 1] → any range

Noise generators return values centred on zero, roughly in [-1, 1]. This is the straight line through (-1, min) and (1, max):

out = min + (n + 1) / 2 · (max − min)

The layout calls it twice per cell. Once for height, into [0, max_height] (default 20). Once for the cell's corner radius, into [nominal · min_ratio, nominal · max_ratio] (default 0.2 to 3.6 for a nominal radius of 4). With the default ratios every radius stays below the nominal radius, so every cell is surrounded by gap. The centres sit 6.93 apart at the default.

{{f1}}
1.2 edge_cuboid_transformvectors + one rotationwhere to put a cuboid that lies along a segment

For drawing a segment as a solid bar, for example a cell edge as a 3D beam. Given the segment's ends from and to, it returns where to put a cuboid (a box) that lies along it:

  • the midpoint, (from + to) / 2
  • the length, |to − from|
  • the rotation that turns +X onto the segment's direction

Start from a unit cube centred on the origin: scale it by length along X, rotate, then move it to the midpoint. The crate exports this for consumers and doesn't call it itself.

{{f2}}
1.3 gap_vertex_datatranslate + cross productshift to origin, normal from a cross product

This prepares a gap polygon for mesh upload. It moves the polygon so its first vertex is at the origin, and finds which way it faces.

The face direction is the cross product of the vectors v0→v1 and v0→v2. For these gaps the winding makes that cross product point down, so the function negates it.

When flat is set it ignores the slope and returns straight up, so tilted gap pieces get the same lighting as the flat faces.

{{f3}}
1.4 idw_interpolate_heightinverse distance weightingheight at any point as a 1/d²-weighted mean of nearby corners

IDW stands for inverse distance weighting. It answers "how high is the ground at an arbitrary point Q?" Every nearby corner votes with its height, and its vote counts 1 / d², where d is its distance to Q in the x–z plane. The closer the corner, the louder its vote:

h(Q) = Σ hᵢ / dᵢ² ⁄ Σ 1 / dᵢ²

Near a corner its weight blows up, so the function simply snaps to that corner once d² < 0.001. It returns None for an empty list and leaves the fallback to the caller.

{{f4}}
1.5 quad_corner_indiceshexx direction tableswhich corners form the gap quad across an edge

Here the lattice itself comes in. hexx numbers a cell's six corners and edges with separate tables (VertexDirection, EdgeDirection).

The gap quad between a cell and its neighbour across edge e has two corners on each cell. On the neighbour, that edge is walked in the opposite direction. The function returns the four indices in a fixed order, (v0, v1, n0, n1), so the quad always comes out as [v0, n0, n1, v1].

edgefaces neighbourowner corners v0, v1neighbour corners n0, n1
0(+1, 0)0, 14, 3
2(−1, +1)2, 30, 5
4(0, −1)4, 52, 1

Callers pass only 0, 2 and 4, because of the quad ownership rule.

{{f5}}
1.6 gap_fillerownership countingcounts the gap quads and junction triangles a set of cells produces

gap_filler counts what the ownership rules produce for any set of cells: one quad per even edge whose neighbour exists, one junction triangle per corner 0 or 1 whose two other cells exist. A unit test (quad_bridge_edges_count_matches_gap_filler) checks its quad count against quad_bridge_edges.

2 · Resolution functions from hexx

hexx (0.24, hex/mod.rs) can treat a radius-r hexagon of cells as one cell of a coarser hex grid. It calls these resolutions, after Sander Evers' "hexagon tiling of a hexagonal grid". The chunk module builds on two of them: a chunk with coordinate c holds the cells with hex.to_lower_res(r) == c, and its centre cell is c.to_higher_res(r). The other functions below are hexx background; the crate doesn't call them.

R.1 Hex::range_countcells in a radius-r hexagon

range_count(r) = 3·r·(r + 1) + 1

Ring k has 6k cells, and summing over rings plus the centre gives this. For r = 2 it's 19, for r = 6 it's 127, and for a radius-60 grid it's 10,981. In to_lower_res this number is called the area: one coarse cell holds exactly this many fine cells.

R.2 Hex::to_higher_reschunk coordinate → its centre cell, linear

Going down to finer cells is a plain linear map in cubic coordinates (x, y, z = −x − y):

to_higher_res((x, y), r) = ( x·(r+1) − r·z , y·(r+1) − r·x )

Chunk (1,0) goes to (2r+1, −r), which is (5,−2) for r = 2. Every chunk centre is an integer combination of two such basis vectors. The coarse lattice is the fine one scaled by √range_count(r) and rotated, so chunk rows never line up with cell rows.

The function is const and exact integer arithmetic.

{{f12}}
R.3 Hex::to_local and wrap_in_rangeoffset inside the chunk; a wraparound world

to_local(h, r) = h − to_higher_res(to_lower_res(h, r), r)

This gives a cell's position relative to its chunk's centre, and it's always within distance r. Together with the chunk coordinate it recovers h.

wrap_in_range(r) is literally to_local(r), read as "fold the plane onto one hexagon around the origin".

{{f13}}
R.4 Hex::to_lower_rescell → chunk coordinate, the hexmod

Going up to coarse cells has to answer "which hexagon contains me?" for a hexagonal tiling. A plain division can't do that. hexx does it in two floor steps over cubic coordinates, with area = range_count(r) and shift = 3r + 2:

X = ⌊(y + shift·x) / area⌋
Y = ⌊(z + shift·y) / area⌋
Z = ⌊(x + shift·z) / area⌋

chunk = ( ⌊(1 + X − Y) / 3⌋ , ⌊(1 + Y − Z) / 3⌋ )

Step 1 projects the cell onto three axes, each skewed by shift so it runs along the rotated coarse lattice, and counts how many area-wide bands it has crossed. That places the cell in one triangle of a triangular grid. Step 2 is ordinary hex rounding of that triangle onto the hexagon that owns it. Worked through for h = (4,0), r = 2 (area 19, shift 8):

stepvalue
cubic (x, y, z)(4, 0, −4)
X = ⌊(0 + 8·4) / 19⌋⌊32/19⌋ = 1
Y = ⌊(−4 + 8·0) / 19⌋⌊−4/19⌋ = −1
Z = ⌊(4 + 8·(−4)) / 19⌋⌊−28/19⌋ = −2
q = ⌊(1 + X − Y) / 3⌋⌊3/3⌋ = 1
r = ⌊(1 + Y − Z) / 3⌋⌊2/3⌋ = 0
chunk(1, 0) ✓ matches figure R.3

A limit for large coordinates: hexx computes y + shift·x in i32, then divides as f32. Integers are exact in f32 only up to 2²⁴ = 16,777,216. Past about 16.7M / (3r + 2) cells from the origin (roughly 840,000 cells for r = 6), the floor can land on the wrong side of a band edge, and a cell can be put in the wrong chunk. HGridLayout::for_chunk and entangled_seam_keys reject such chunks with ChunkError::TooFarFromOrigin.

3 · What one cell owns

HGridLayout::new builds a HexagonalMap of radius settings.radius around Hex::ZERO. Each cell gets its height and radius from Fbm<Perlin> sampled at its world centre divided by a scale (50 for height, 30 for radius).

  • A cell's height and radius come from noise at its world position, unless pinned through overrides. Its neighbours don't affect them, and neither does the size or centre of the grid. That is why a chunk can be built on its own (section 5).
  • Corners scale with cell.radius; centres don't (see terms). So a smaller cell widens the gaps around it, but never moves.

4 · Entangled cells

A chunk layout (section 5) holds one ring of cells more than the chunk: its neighbours' cells at distance r + 1 from the centre, so every gap along the chunk's edge has both sides. Those are the entangled cells (is_entangled(hex)). Being entangled is not something a cell stores; it follows from where the cell sits. A HGridLayout::new grid has none.

  • An entangled cell emits no face: its own chunk draws that.
  • A gap quad between two entangled cells is skipped: it touches no cell of the chunk.
  • Every other gap piece an entangled cell owns is kept. Those are the chunk's entangled seam tris.

So all_tris() on a chunk layout draws the chunk's faces and every gap piece that reaches an own cell, whoever owns it. The outline is closed on all six sides:

5 · Streaming chunks

Any chunk of an unbounded world is built on demand from its coordinate, with one set of settings (and so one set of seeds) for the whole world.

A chunk is the radius-r hexagon of cells with hex.to_lower_res(r) == coord (section 2). HGridLayout::for_chunk(g, coord, r, overrides) builds it with one extra entangled ring of neighbour cells at distance r + 1. It samples noise at world positions, so a chunk built alone matches its neighbours exactly, then applies overrides: the stored edits of the chunk's own cells and of its ring. Layout functions then cut it into two streams:

  • own: own_tris(), what the chunk's own cells own, 14 triangles per cell in own_cells() order. Across all chunks the own streams cover the world with no overlap and no holes.
  • entangled seams: entangled_seam_tris(), the gaps that ring cells own and that touch the chunk. Each is a copy of a triangle in the owning neighbour's own stream. entangled_seam_keys(coord, r) names that owner for each one, from hex coordinates alone. A junction triangle at a chunk corner touches a third chunk too, which carries the same copy, so its key also names that chunk (EntangledSeam { owner, also }).
{{f14}}

Rules for consumers, from the module docs:

  1. Always draw the own stream. Draw a seam triangle only while its owner isn't loaded. If it names a third chunk, draw it only while that chunk isn't loaded either, or comes after this one in (q, r) order. Every triangle touching a loaded chunk is then drawn exactly once (EntangledSeam::drawn; its test cases are in testdata/entangled_seam_drawn.tsv).
  2. Entangled cells are never state. A change to a cell is stored under its owning chunk only, and passed as an override to every layout that holds it.
  3. A changed cell rebuilds its own chunk and every chunk that has it in its entangled ring: 1 chunk for an interior cell, 2 along a chunk edge, 3 at a chunk corner.

In the browser, chunk_own_tris, chunk_entangled_seam_tris and chunk_entangled_seam_keys hand out the same streams as flat buffers, one call per chunk. The chunk treadmill keeps seven chunks loaded around a moving camera and follows rule 1 on every step.
